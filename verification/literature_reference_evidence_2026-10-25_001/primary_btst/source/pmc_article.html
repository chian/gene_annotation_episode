ABSTRACT

The peptide transporter carbon starvation (CstA) family (transporter classification [TC] 2.A.114) belongs to the second largest superfamily of secondary transporters, the amino acid/polyamine/organocation (APC) superfamily. No representative of the CstA family has previously been characterized either biochemically or structurally, but we have now identified the function of one of its members, the transport protein YjiY of Escherichia coli. Expression of the yjiY gene is regulated by the LytS-like histidine kinase BtsS, a sensor of extracellular pyruvate, together with the LytTR-like response regulator BtsR. YjiY consists of 716 amino acids, which form 18 putative transmembrane helices. Transport studies with intact cells provided evidence that YjiY is a specific and high-affinity transporter for pyruvate (Km, 16 μM). Furthermore, reconstitution of the purified YjiY into proteoliposomes revealed that YjiY is a pyruvate/H+ symporter. It has long been assumed that E. coli possesses a transporter(s) for pyruvate, but the present study is the first to definitively identify such a protein. Based on its function, we propose to change the name of the uncharacterized gene yjiY to btsT for Brenztraubensäure (the German word for pyruvate) transporter.

IMPORTANCE BtsT (formerly known as YjiY) is found in many commensal and pathogenic representatives of the Enterobacteriaceae. This study for the first time characterizes a pyruvate transporter in E. coli, BtsT, as a specific pyruvate/H+ symporter. When nutrients are limiting, BtsT takes up pyruvate from the medium, thus enabling it to be used as a carbon source for the growth and survival of E. coli.

KEYWORDS: CstA family, overflow metabolism, proteoliposomes, secondary transporter, starvation

INTRODUCTION

The protein YjiY belongs to a small family of transporters named for the peptide transporter carbon starvation CstA (transporter classification [TC] 2.A.114) (1). The CstA family is part of the amino acid/polyamine/organocation (APC) superfamily of secondary transporters (2). The latter is the second largest superfamily of secondary transporters, and its representatives cover a broad spectrum of substrates, ranging from amino acids to metal ions and peptides (2). Within the APC transporters, the CstA family displays the highest diversity in sequence and topology. Some members of the CstA family are already characterized: CstA of Escherichia coli (3), CstA of Campylobacter jejuni (4), CstA and YjiY of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (5). In both species, E. coli and C. jejuni, the gene cstA is upregulated under carbon starvation, and knockout mutants have a lower growth rate in the presence of peptides as a nitrogen source (3, 6). Furthermore, a cstA or a yjiY mutant of S. Typhimurium is impaired in the utilization of several dipeptides (5). However, no representative of the CstA family has yet been biochemically analyzed to ascertain the identity of its substrate(s).

Our studies focus on YjiY, which displays high sequence similarity (75.4%) and identity (61.1%) to CstA from E. coli (7). The corresponding gene yjiY is the sole target gene of the histidine kinase/response regulator system BtsS/BtsR, and its expression leads to the synthesis of a 716-amino-acid protein (7). In the E. coli genome yjiY is disconnected from the yehUT operon. However, in other bacterial genomes these genes are adjacent to each other (7, 8), which suggests that they might be functionally related (7). Expression of yjiY is additionally regulated by cyclic AMP receptor protein (CRP) and posttranscriptionally modulated by CsrA (7).

It has been hypothesized that YjiY is involved in nutrient uptake, most probably the transport of pyruvate (9). This assumption is supported by proteome studies of E. coli under different growth conditions, which demonstrated that in cells grown in minimal medium with pyruvate as a carbon source, levels of YjiY are up to 74-fold higher than those of cells grown in amino acid-rich LB medium (10). Additionally, a quantitative fitness screen of bar-coded E. coli BW25113 transposon mutants revealed that the yjiY mutant displays reduced fitness when grown in minimal medium containing pyruvate as the sole carbon source (11). Furthermore, pyruvate is vital to most living cells, as a source of both energy and carbon, since it forms the central node of carbon metabolism (together with phosphoenolpyruvate) in E. coli (12). Under conditions that require increased consumption of organic compounds during exponential growth, Escherichia coli excretes acetate as well as pyruvate via overflow metabolism (13). In later phases of growth, the extracellular pyruvate is then scavenged by the cells (14). It was shown that BtsS/BtsR responds to such external pyruvate by activating the expression of yjiY (9). The histidine kinase BtsS is a high-affinity receptor for extracellular pyruvate (9). It is therefore tempting to speculate that YjiY is a pyruvate transporter.

To date, little is known about pyruvate transport in E. coli. It has been shown that a separate transport system for pyruvate exists in E. coli (15) and that cells possess an active uptake system for pyruvate (16). However, pyruvate transport has not been assigned to any specific gene product. A gene located at around centisome 15 on the E. coli gene map has been suggested to encode an inducible pyruvate transport system (17) but has not been further characterized. Uptake of pyruvate has also been shown in Rhodopseudomonas sphaeroides (18) and Lactobacillus plantarum (19), but the transport systems responsible have not been defined. MctC in Corynebacterium glutamicum (20), MctP in Rhizobium leguminosarum (21), and TRAP-T in Anabaena sp. (22) have all been characterized to function as pyruvate transporters. However, the specificity of these transporters is not restricted to pyruvate. MctC, a transporter that requires the electrochemical proton gradient, prefers acetate and propionate and has a low affinity for pyruvate (K0.5, 250 μM). MctP, a sodium/solute transporter, has a high affinity for pyruvate (Km, 3.8 μM) but shows a rather broad substrate specificity (alanine, lactate, pyruvate and probably also propionate, acetate, butyrate, and α-hydroxybutyrate). The tripartite ATP-independent periplasmic transport system (TRAP-T) takes up several monocarboxylate 2-oxoacids, including pyruvate (Km, 67 μM).

This study represents the first to be devoted to defining the function of YjiY. Analyses with intact cells demonstrated that YjiY is able to transport pyruvate with a high affinity and specificity. In addition, the reconstitution of purified YjiY in proteoliposomes proved that pyruvate transport requires the proton motive force for energization. To indicate its role as a pyruvate transporter in E. coli and highlight its functional relationship with the pyruvate-responsive BtsS/BtsR histidine kinase/response regulator system (9), YjiY has been renamed BtsT (for Brenztraubensäure transporter) and is so referred to in what follows.

RESULTS

BtsT-like proteins in Enterobacteriaceae.

BtsT belongs to the CstA family, which consists of 10 members in the Transporter Classification Database (www.tcdb.org), all of which share a high degree of sequence conservation (1). We were interested in elucidating the relationship between the BtsT homologs found among the Enterobacteriaceae by comparative genomics. For this purpose, we performed a local alignment search based on the full-length amino acid sequences using Protein BLAST (23). Based on the alignment of 1,617 individually identified sequences, we generated a phylogenetic tree and grouped the corresponding proteins (see Data Set S1 in the supplemental material). The tree possesses three distinct branches, which were named BtsT-like, CstA1-like, and CstA2-like (Fig. 1), taking the two known BtsT and CstA proteins in E. coli as branch markers. Within the Enterobacteriaceae, 43.5% of all sequences were assigned to the BtsT-like branch of the tree and 40.8%, including E. coli CstA, to the CstA1-like branch. Surprisingly, the remaining 15.8% of the identified sequences formed an additional, distinct branch, named CstA2-like. The genus Escherichia contained examples of both BtsT and CstA1, while other genera like Dickeya and Serratia possessed representatives of both the CstA1 and CstA2 branches. No single genus contained representatives of all three branches. Although the amino acid sequences show an extraordinarily high level of identity (e.g., 61.1% between BtsT and CstA in E. coli), CstA sequences differ sufficiently to be attributed to separate branches. Furthermore, the frequent presence of more than one CstA representative within the same species suggests that members of different branches might have different functions within the Enterobacteriaceae.

Since BtsT (YjiY) of S. enterica was proposed to function as a peptide transporter (5), we compared the sequences of BtsT of E. coli and S. enterica using a consensus-based approach. A consensus derived from 148 BtsT sequences of E. coli was aligned to a consensus of 122 BtsT sequences of S. enterica (Fig. S1). The sequence identity was determined to be 97%. Most of the deviating amino acids are located in the loops of BtsT. Although the difference in sequence identity is rather small, a single amino acid substitution might be sufficient to alter the substrate specificity. It is worth mentioning that there are differences in the metabolic behavior of these two species. While E. coli excretes pyruvate during growth in an amino acid-rich medium as part of an overflow metabolism, S. enterica did not excrete pyruvate to the same extent (24). In addition, in E. coli the histidine kinase/response regulator system BtsS/BtsR (its target gene is btsT) together with the histidine kinase/response regulator system YpdA/YpdB forms a regulatory network (25). The YpdA/YpdB system is not encoded in S. enterica. Here, we elucidate the function of BtsT in E. coli.

Secondary structure model of BtsT.

CstA family members contain 13 to 18 transmembrane domains (TMs), which are organized into two central 5-TM repeat units with 1 to 4 extra TMs on each side. The first helix of the second repeat unit (helix 10 in Fig. 2) contains a conserved motif CG-X2-SG of unknown function (2). The structural similarity between BtsT and secondary transporters of the APC superfamily (TM4 to TM17) was confirmed by Phyre2, a protein homology/analogy recognition engine (26). Furthermore, hydropathy analyses of BtsT with UniProt predicted that the membrane-integrated portion contains 17 putative TMs while CstA displays 18 TMs (Fig. S2) (27).

Since the C terminus of BtsT is located on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane (28), the odd number of domains implied that the N terminus of BtsT should extend into the periplasm. Because a periplasmically located N terminus is uncommon among integral membrane proteins, we investigated its location in BtsT by using the MalE fusion strategy (29). Native MalE is a periplasmic maltose-binding protein (MBP), and it contains a leader sequence that enables it to be translocated through the cytoplasmic membrane. MalE without the leader sequence is produced as cytoplasmic protein (30). We fused malE with or without a leader sequence to the 5′ end of btsT to code for either a periplasmically located MBP (MBPP-BtsT) or a cytoplasmically located MBP (MBPC-BtsT). We predicted that only a correctly folded hybrid protein would be inserted into the cytoplasmic membrane. We overproduced both hybrid proteins individually in E. coli and determined the level and localization of each by Western blotting. The MBPC-BtsT was found predominantly in the membrane fraction (Fig. S3A). The level of MBPP-BtsT was low, and no hybrid protein was detectable in any cellular subfraction (Fig. S3B). In addition, we performed complementation studies using the MBP-deficient E. coli mutant MM39, which grows only with maltose as a sole carbon source, when it produces a periplasmically located MBP (Fig. S4). E. coli MM39 producing either MBPP-BtsT or MBPC-BtsT was unable to grow, corroborating previous results (Fig. S3). These results revealed that only BtsT with a cytoplasmically fused MBP is correctly integrated into the cytoplasmic membrane, which therefore suggests that the N terminus of BtsT is located on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane.

Then we compared the predicted secondary structures for BtsT and CstA and found a long loop between TM11 and TM12 in BtsT (Fig. S2A). According to PSIPRED secondary structure prediction (31), amino acids 417 to 437 in this loop should have a high propensity for forming a helix and therefore a TM. Based on the experimental evidence for the location of the N terminus and the structure prediction for CstA, we propose a secondary structure model of BtsT with an intracellular N terminus and 18 TMs (Fig. 2).

BtsT activity in intact cells.

Since pyruvate is an important metabolite of central carbon metabolism and other pyruvate transport systems in E. coli are yet unknown, we started to work with a mutant strain deficient only in btsT to analyze BtsT activity in intact cells. It is important to note that there were no growth defects for mutant MG1655 ΔbtsT either in LB medium (Fig. S5A) or in M9 minimal medium supplemented with pyruvate (Fig. S5B). Uptake of [14C]pyruvate into intact cells of E. coli MG1655 ΔbtsT (25) transformed with either pBAD24-btsT (7) or pBAD24 (32) was analyzed by a rapid filtration assay. An initial experiment showed that it is difficult to measure [14C]pyruvate due to extremely fast uptake and/or metabolization (data not shown). Therefore, all subsequent assays were performed at 15°C. At this lower temperature, the rate of uptake of [14C]pyruvate by E. coli MG1655 ΔbtsT containing the control plasmid pBAD24 was approximately linear for 60 s (Fig. 3). Cells transformed with pBAD24-btsT displayed a 6-fold higher linear uptake rate for 30 s and reached 4-fold higher steady state (0.5 nmol/mg of protein) at about 40 s. Transport rates in intact cells of E. coli MG1655 ΔbtsT transformed with pBAD24-btsT were also measured at various external pH values (Fig. S6). The highest pyruvate uptake rate was found at pH 7.5, the pH value of the buffer used in all of the subsequent experiments. The pyruvate uptake rate decreased at pH 6, and no transport was detected at pH 4.5 or pH 3.0.

In addition, we tested pyruvate uptake by BtsT in intact cells, which have a decreased pyruvate metabolism. For this purpose, we used E. coli strain YYC202, which harbors mutations in the pyruvate metabolizing pathways aceEF, pflB, poxB, and pps, and therefore cannot convert pyruvate into either acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA), phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), or acetate (33). Uptake of [14C]pyruvate into intact cells of E. coli YYC202 transformed with either pBAD24-btsT or pBAD24 was measured, and we observed a pattern of pyruvate uptake very similar to that in E. coli MG1655 ΔbtsT transformed with these plasmids (Fig. S7). Therefore, accumulation of pyruvate could not be increased in this mutant, since it is impossible to completely block its metabolization. It is important to state here that expression of chromosomally encoded btsT is tightly controlled by the BtsS/BtsR system and therefore requires specific environmental conditions (9).

Characterization of pyruvate transport by BtsT in intact cells.

Previous observations based on the effects of electron transport chain inhibitors on pyruvate transport in E. coli K-12 suggested that the process is driven by the proton motive force (16). To test this hypothesis, we used the hydrophobic protonophores 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) and carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) (Fig. 4A). Both reagents abolished substrate accumulation by BtsT, suggesting active transport of pyruvate by BtsT. To determine whether pyruvate transport by BtsT is accompanied by the movement of other ion species, several ionophores were tested. Valinomycin is a highly selective ionophore for K+ (34), while nonactin forms complexes with K+, Na+, NH4+, and some other cations with lower affinity (35). Nigericin acts mainly as a potassium-proton antiporter but can also transport Pb+ and H+ into the cytosol (36). Pyruvate uptake by BtsT was not affected by any of these ionophores (Fig. 4A).

Additionally, the specificity of BtsT was tested by assaying several compounds with shared structural similarity for the ability to act as competitive inhibitors for the pyruvate binding site when provided in a 100-fold access (Fig. 4B). Br-pyruvate is a synthetic analogue of pyruvate, and was found to act as a competitive inhibitor of BtsT, which is in agreement with earlier studies in E. coli K-12 (16). None of the other compounds used (l-malate, l-lactate, and d-lactate with a C-α hydroxyl group; phosphoenolpyruvate [PEP] with a C-α phosphoryl group; or l-alanine with a positively charged C-α amino group) reduced the rate of pyruvate uptake. We also tested monocarboxylates (acetate and propionate), a dicarboxylate (succinate), and amino acids (l-serine and l-glycine), but the uptake rate was significantly reduced only in the presence of pyruvate or its synthetic analog Br-pyruvate, indicating the narrow specificity of the transporter. In order to determine the Km for pyruvate uptake, we quantified the initial rate of pyruvate uptake by BtsT at different concentrations of pyruvate (Fig. 4C). Pyruvate can enter the cells by diffusion (Fig. S8). Therefore, the uptake rates were corrected by the values determined for pure diffusion. The Km of the transporter BtsT for pyruvate measured in intact cells was 16.5 ± 6.4 μM.

Effects of Δμ̃H+, ΔΨ, and ΔpH on BtsT-mediated pyruvate transport.

Energization of pyruvate transport by BtsT was studied in detail in E. coli proteoliposomes by determining the consequences for pyruvate transport of an imposed electrical potential (ΔΨ) across the liposome membrane or a difference in proton concentration (ΔpH) between the interior of the liposomes and the external medium, or a combination of both, thus generating a transmembrane electrochemical proton gradient (Δμ̃H+). To this end, BtsT was first expressed at high levels under the control of the pBAD promoter, affinity purified, and integrated into proteoliposomes preloaded with potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.6 (37) (Fig. S9). The proteoliposomes were then diluted 200-fold, in the presence of valinomycin, with either potassium phosphate buffer, pH 5.8 (ΔpH), potassium-free buffer, pH 7.6 (ΔΨ), potassium-free buffer, pH 5.8 (Δμ̃H+), or sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.6 (Δμ̃Na+), and the rates of [14C]pyruvate transport were measured.

From the results, it is clear that the presence of an electrochemical H+ gradient (Δμ̃H+) is associated with the accumulation of [14C]pyruvate in proteoliposomes and that uptake is markedly reduced in the absence of such a gradient (dilution in potassium phosphate buffer pH 7.6) (Fig. 5A). Thus, the initial rate of uptake of pyruvate by BtsT in the presence of Δμ̃H+ is 27.7 nmol per mg of protein, and the steady state is achieved after about 1 min (Fig. 5B). Imposition of ΔpH decreased the initial rate by 2-fold, and a lower steady state (relative to Δμ̃H+) was reached after 1 min. In the presence of ΔΨ, we observed an initial rate of pyruvate accumulation similar to that seen in the presence of ΔpH, but it continued to rise for the (10-min) duration of the experiment. [14C]pyruvate also accumulated upon the application of Δμ̃Na+, although the Na+ ions did not stimulate initial uptake beyond the level seen with ΔpH alone (Fig. 5A).

Pyruvate could potentially cross the E. coli cytoplasmic membrane in its protonated form by simple diffusion (38). In the experiments where a pH gradient is generated (ΔpH, interior alkaline, exterior acidic), the [14C]pyruvate is added to an extracellular environment with a low pH. Therefore, [14C]pyruvate might be protonated and transported by diffusion. To rule out this possibility, liposomes without BtsT were prepared as a control (Fig. S10). Using these liposomes, we observed only a slight pyruvate accumulation. Therefore, the protonation of pyruvate at low pH does not significantly affect the outcome of these experiments.

Lastly, we confirmed the functionality of the transporter BtsT in proteoliposomes in a counterflow assay. In this case, we preloaded the proteoliposomes with a high internal concentration (10 mM) of unlabeled (“cold”) pyruvate and subsequently diluted them 200-fold in the same buffer containing a low concentration of [14C]pyruvate (4.7 μM). If pyruvate was indeed transported, exchange of the internal (unlabeled) substrate and external (labeled) pyruvate should lead to the accumulation of [14C]pyruvate in the vesicles due to the concentration gradient across the membrane. We observed such an accumulation of [14C]pyruvate via BtsT in proteoliposomes, which was low (0.28 nmol × mg−1 × min−1) but significant in light of the competitive inhibition by unlabeled pyruvate (Fig. 5C). In the control experiment, proteoliposomes preloaded with 10 mM unlabeled lactate exhibited no [14C]pyruvate accumulation. These experiments thus demonstrate that BtsT-mediated uptake of pyruvate can occur against a concentration gradient.

DISCUSSION

Pyruvate plays a central role in carbon metabolism. Nevertheless, little is known about how intracellular levels of the compound are regulated in E. coli. It has been suggested that pyruvate uptake is driven via specific active transporters (16, 17), but until now their molecular identities have remained unknown.

However, interest in pyruvate transporters is growing, primarily as a result of increasing evidence for their roles in biological fitness and virulence of Enterobacteriaceae. The pyruvate-tricarboxylic acid cycle node was identified as a focal point for controlling the host colonization and virulence of Yersinia pseudotuberculosis (39). Y. pseudotuberculosis secretes large amounts of pyruvate when grown in minimal medium with glucose as the carbon source. However, the pyruvate exporter or importer in Y. pseudotuberculosis has not yet been characterized.

Metabolic engineering is also focusing its attention on pyruvate in efforts to achieve optimized metabolite production in E. coli, C. glutamicum, or Bacillus subtilis (40). However, to control the carbon flux between glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, further insights into the regulation and characterization of the enzymes and other proteins relevant to the phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)-pyruvate-oxaloacetate node are still required.

Here, we were principally interested in elucidating the role of the pyruvate transporter in E. coli. It is known that pyruvate overflow occurs under conditions of carbon excess during exponential growth. At the end of the exponential phase, cells experience nutrient limitation and initiate the rapid reuptake of previously excreted metabolic intermediates, including pyruvate (13). This extracellular pyruvate is sensed by the two-component system BtsS/BtsR, which consequently activates the expression of btsT encoding the putative pyruvate transporter BtsT (7, 9). BtsT belongs to the APC superfamily of secondary transporters (2, 41). Comparative genomic studies clearly separate BtsT from CstA, and it has been proposed that the two have different functions (Fig. 1). Since the histidine kinase BtsS responds to extracellular pyruvate (9), we hypothesized that BtsS/BtsR triggers the production of BtsT transporter to enable cells to take up pyruvate as a scavenging strategy to cope with carbon limitation. Here, we have shown that BtsT indeed serves as a pyruvate transporter in E. coli and thus assigned a function to a representative of the CstA family for the first time.

First we studied the function of BtsT in intact cells and characterized the protein as a highly specific pyruvate transporter with a Km of 16 μM (Fig. 4). Furthermore, we showed that the protonophores CCCP and DNP had a significant inhibitory effect on pyruvate transport rates, indicating involvement of ΔΨ and/or ΔpH. In comparison, neither valinomycin nor nigericin caused inhibition of pyruvate uptake by BtsT, even though these compounds dissipate either ΔΨ or ΔpH, respectively. Likewise, valinomycin had no effect on pyruvate transport by L. plantarum cells (19). The lack of an inhibitory effect of valinomycin suggests that intact cells somehow circumvent inhibition by a thus far unknown mechanism. Elimination of all the influences from intact cells is therefore required to obtain reliable insights into the energization of pyruvate transport.

It is worth mentioning that E. coli probably harbors at least one additional pyruvate transporter, based on the observed background pyruvate accumulation by intact cells of an E. coli ΔbtsT strain (Fig. 3), which cannot be solely attributed to diffusion (see Fig. S6 and S8 in the supplemental material). It was previously suggested that E. coli uses one export and two uptake transporters to modulate the level of intracellular pyruvate (17). Unfortunately, we were unable to identify other pyruvate transporters based on the BtsT sequence, and contrary to a previous suggestion (17), we could find no BtsT-like coding sequence near the centisome 15 position in the E. coli genome.

Further insights into the mode of action of BtsT were obtained by incorporating the protein into E. coli proteoliposomes. In these experiments, a suspension of proteliposomes harboring BtsT was assayed for [14C]pyruvate transport under conditions known to generate either Δμ̃H+, ΔΨ, or ΔpH (Fig. 5). From the results, it is obvious that simultaneous imposition of a membrane potential and a proton gradient (Δμ̃H+, interior negative and alkaline) is required to drive pyruvate uptake into E. coli proteoliposomes. In the presence of either ΔΨ (interior negative) or ΔpH (interior alkaline), the intial rate of transport was half of that observed in the presence of Δμ̃H+. The two gradients most likely contribute with their actual extent to the energization of uptake (Fig. 5). Therefore, it seems that the secondary transport of pyruvate by BtsT is coupled to both the influx of protons and the movement of electric charges. Hence, BtsT acts as a pyruvate/H+ symporter. Although the exact pyruvate/H+ coupling stoichiometry is still unknown, the results suggest a ratio of more than one proton per transported pyruvate.

In summary, BtsT is the first identified pyruvate transporter in E. coli and the first characterized representative of the CstA family. This study opens the possibility of searching for the pyruvate-binding site in BtsT as well as elucidating the three-dimensional structure of this transporter. It will be interesting to determine which of the 18 putative TMs in BtsT are essential for the recognition and transport of pyruvate. Currently, we can only speculate that the two conserved 5-TM repeat units found in members of the CstA family (2) are crucial for this function. BtsT contains 8 additional TMs (4 TMs before and 4 after the repeat unit), which might perhaps interact with the histidine kinase BtsS (25) or have other regulatory or structural functions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial strains and plasmids.

In this study, we used the previously described E. coli strains LMG194 [F− ΔlacX74 galE galK thi rpsL ΔphoA Δara714 leu::Tn10] (32), MG1655 ΔbtsT (previously termed MG1655 ΔyjiY) (25), MM39 (araD lacI ΔU1269 malE444 StrepR) (29), and YYC202 (DSM 14335 Hfr zbi::Tn10 poxB1 Δ(aceEF) rpsL pps-4 pfl-1) (33) and constructed two new plasmids: pMALP-btsT and pMALc-btsT. The corresponding btsT-6His gene was isolated from plasmid pBAD24-btsT (previously known as pBAD24-yjiY [7]) by cleavage with EcoRI and XbaI and ligated into the cognate sites in vectors pMAL-p2X and pMAL-c2X (New England BioLabs). For transport studies we used the arabinose-inducible vector pBAD24-btsT (7), which codes for BtsT-6His, and pBAD24 (32) as control.

Growth conditions.

All strains were grown overnight in LB medium (10 g/liter tryptone, 5 g/liter yeast extract, 10 g/liter NaCl) or M9 minimal medium containing 0.5% (wt/vol) maltose or 20 mM pyruvate. Cells from the overnight culture were then transferred to corresponding fresh medium. When appropriate, media were supplemented with ampicillin (sodium salt, 100 μg/ml) and streptomycin (50 μg/ml) as required. After inoculation, bacteria were grown under agitation (200 rpm) at 37°C and growth was monitored over time by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600).

Production of MBP hybrid proteins.

Cells were grown to an OD600 of 0.5 as described above. Recombinant gene expression was induced by addition of 0.2% (wt/vol) arabinose. After 3 h of further incubation, cells were harvested by centrifugation, disrupted (using a high pressure cell disrupter [Constant Systems]) and fractionated. At each step, the pellet was resuspended in a buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.5), 10% (vol/vol) glycerol, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and DNase. His-tagged BtsT was detected by Western blotting with an anti-His antibody (Abcam) and an alkaline phosphatase-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (Rockland) as the secondary antibody.

Comparative genomic studies.

BtsT-like proteins were identified by Protein BLAST search within the Enterobacteriaceae (taxonomic identifier [taxid] 543) using the full-length amino acid sequence of E. coli BtsT to query the RefSeq protein database (23). In order to cover a high number of highly diverse proteins an expected (E) value was kept at <10. An amino acid length tolerance was set to 10% as default parameter resulting in 1,617 sequences. To elucidate the relationship between the BtsT homologs, all sequences were aligned and a phylogenetic tree was generated using CLC Main Workbench 7 (Qiagen Bioinformatics). BtsT and CstA of E. coli were used as branch markers. Comparison of BtsT conserved in different species, E. coli and S. enterica, was done via a consensus alignment. For this purpose, a total of 148 sequences of BtsT in E. coli (or 122 in S. enterica) was retrieved from the branch BtsT of the phylogenetic tree. The consensus sequence was derived from their alignment using CLC Main Workbench 7 software.

Transport measurements with intact cells.

E. coli strain MG1655 ΔbtsT (25) was transformed with pBAD24 (32) or pBAD24-btsT (7). Cells grown in LB medium in the absence of arabinose (the leaky expression of btsT was suitable for complementation) were harvested in mid-log phase. Cells were washed twice and resuspended in 100 mM Tris/MES (morpholineethanesulfonic acid) buffer (pH 7.5) containing 5 mM MgCl2, thereby adjusting the total protein concentration to 0.35 mg/ml. In the experiments performed at different external pH values, the cells were washed and resuspended in buffers: Tris-MES buffer (pH 6), 5 mM MgCl2, or 100 mM citrate buffer (pH 3/pH 4.5), MgCl2. Uptake of [14C]pyruvate (55 mCi mmol−1 [Biotrend]) was measured at a total substrate concentration of 10 μM at 15°C. At various time intervals, transport was terminated by the addition of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) and 100 mM LiCl (stop buffer) followed by rapid filtration through membrane filters (MN gf-5 0.4 μm; Macherey-Nagel). The filters were dissolved in 5 ml of scintillation fluid (MP Biomedicals), and radioactivity was determined in a liquid scintillation analyzer (PerkinElmer). Effects of the cold substrates on pyruvate uptake by BtsT were tested by simultaneous addition of cold compound (1 mM) and [14C]pyruvate (10 μM). The effects of protonophores and ionophores were tested after preincubation of cells in Tris-MES buffer (pH 7.5) supplemented with 2 mM DNP, 20 μM CCCP, 6 μM nigericin, 10 μM nonactin or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (as a control) at 25°C for 30 min. In the case of the ionophore valinomycin cells were preincubated in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.5, at 25°C for 30 min. In experiments where the pyruvate concentration was varied, the amount of [14C]pyruvate was kept constant (9 nmol).

Transport measurements with proteoliposomes.

E. coli LMG194 cells were transformed with pBAD24-btsT (7), and BtsT-6His was produced as described above. At each step, the pellet was resuspended in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.6) containing 5 mM MgCl2, 5% (vol/vol) glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, and DNase. For BtsT solubilization, 1.5% (wt/vol) n-dodecyl β-d-maltoside (Glycon Biochemicals) was added during stirring on ice for 30 min, and the sample was centrifuged at 244,000 × g for 45 min at 4°C. Ni2+-nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) resin, which had been preincubated with buffer W (100 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 7.6], 5 mM MgCl2, 5% [vol/vol] glycerol, and 1 mM DTT), was then added to the supernatant, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h with gentle shaking at 4°C. Unbound protein was removed by washing with buffer W containing 30 mM imidazole. Subsequently, BtsT was eluted with buffer W containing 300 mM imidazole. Purified BtsT was reconstituted into preformed liposomes prepared from an acetone-ether-washed E. coli polar lipid extract as described before (42). Prior to reconstitution, the liposomes were destabilized by the addition of 0.12% (wt/vol) Triton X-100 and then mixed with purified protein at a 100:1 ratio. Detergents were removed by adding Bio-Beads SM-2 (Bio-Rad) at a 5:1 bead/detergent ratio. Fresh Bio-Beads were added after 1 h and 2 h, and the mixture was gently stirred at 4°C overnight. Bio-Beads were removed by filtration through glass wool, and the proteoliposome suspension was dialyzed twice against buffer A (100 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 7.6], 5 mM MgCl2, and 5% [vol/vol] glycerol), and centrifuged at 160,000 × g for 1 h. The pellet was resuspended in buffer A and stored in liquid N2. Proteins were visualized by silver staining, and His-tagged BtsT was detected by Western blotting as described before. After thawing, samples were extruded through a 400-nm filter (Avestin) to obtain unilamellar proteoliposomes of homogeneous size, and adjusted to 4 mg/ml of protein in buffer A. Aliquots of proteoliposomes were diluted (1:200) into buffers A to E containing 80 μM valinomycin and 40 μM [14C]pyruvate (55 mCi mmol−1) to initiate pyruvate transport, and at defined times (25°C) the entire sample was filtered through 0.2-μm Millipore filters (buffer B [ΔpH]: 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 5.8] and 5 mM MgCl2; buffer C [ΔΨ]: 100 mM Tris/MES buffer [pH 7.6] and 5 mM MgCl2; buffer D [Δμ̃H+]: 100 mM Tris/MES buffer [pH 5.8] and 5 mM MgCl2; buffer E [Δμ̃Na+]: 100 mM Tris/MES [pH 5.8], 5 mM MgCl2, and 50 mM NaCl). For the counterflow assay, proteoliposomes were preloaded with 10 mM pyruvate or lactate at 4°C overnight. The resulting suspension was diluted 200-fold into buffer A containing [14C]pyruvate at a concentration of 4.7 μM (final external pyruvate concentration of 54.7 μM) at 25°C. At defined times the entire sample was filtered, and the radioactivity of the sample was analyzed as described above.

Statistical analysis.

All experiments were repeated at least three times with independently prepared samples. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 5.03 for Windows). Data were assessed for significance between tested groups by using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc test. Error bars represent standard errors of the mean.
