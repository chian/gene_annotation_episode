CstA family members contain 13 to 18 transmembrane domains (TMs), which are organized into two central 5-TM repeat units with 1 to 4 extra TMs on each side. The first helix of the second repeat unit (helix 10 in Fig. 2 ) contains a conserved motif CG-X 2 -SG of unknown function ( 2 ). The structural similarity between BtsT and secondary transporters of the APC superfamily (TM4 to TM17) was confirmed by Phyre2, a protein homology/analogy recognition engine ( 26 ). Furthermore, hydropathy analyses of BtsT with UniProt predicted that the membrane-integrated portion contains 17 putative TMs while CstA displays 18 TMs (Fig. S2) ( 27 ).

Since the C terminus of BtsT is located on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane ( 28 ), the odd number of domains implied that the N terminus of BtsT should extend into the periplasm. Because a periplasmically located N terminus is uncommon among integral membrane proteins, we investigated its location in BtsT by using the MalE fusion strategy ( 29 ). Native MalE is a periplasmic maltose-binding protein (MBP), and it contains a leader sequence that enables it to be translocated through the cytoplasmic membrane. MalE without the leader sequence is produced as cytoplasmic protein ( 30 ). We fused malE with or without a leader sequence to the 5′ end of btsT to code for either a periplasmically located MBP (MBP P -BtsT) or a cytoplasmically located MBP (MBP C -BtsT). We predicted that only a correctly folded hybrid protein would be inserted into the cytoplasmic membrane. We overproduced both hybrid proteins individually in E. coli and determined the level and localization of each by Western blotting. The MBP C -BtsT was found predominantly in the membrane fraction (Fig. S3A). The level of MBP P -BtsT was low, and no hybrid protein was detectable in any cellular subfraction (Fig. S3B). In addition, we performed complementation studies using the MBP-deficient E. coli mutant MM39, which grows only with maltose as a sole carbon source, when it produces a periplasmically located MBP (Fig. S4). E. coli MM39 producing either MBP P -BtsT or MBP C -BtsT was unable to grow, corroborating previous results (Fig. S3). These results revealed that only BtsT with a cytoplasmically fused MBP is correctly integrated into the cytoplasmic membrane, which therefore suggests that the N terminus of BtsT is located on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane.
Then we compared the predicted secondary structures for BtsT and CstA and found a long loop between TM11 and TM12 in BtsT (Fig. S2A). According to PSIPRED secondary structure prediction ( 31 ), amino acids 417 to 437 in this loop should have a high propensity for forming a helix and therefore a TM. Based on the experimental evidence for the location of the N terminus and the structure prediction for CstA, we propose a secondary structure model of BtsT with an intracellular N terminus and 18 TMs ( Fig. 2 ).
BtsT activity in intact cells.
Since pyruvate is an important metabolite of central carbon metabolism and other pyruvate transport systems in E. coli are yet unknown, we started to work with a mutant strain deficient only in btsT to analyze BtsT activity in intact cells. It is important to note that there were no growth defects for mutant MG1655 Δ btsT either in LB medium (Fig. S5A) or in M9 minimal medium supplemented with pyruvate (Fig. S5B). Uptake of [ 14 C]pyruvate into intact cells of E. coli MG1655 Δ btsT ( 25 ) transformed with either pBAD24- btsT ( 7 ) or pBAD24 ( 32 ) was analyzed by a rapid filtration assay. An initial experiment showed that it is difficult to measure [ 14 C]pyruvate due to extremely fast uptake and/or metabolization (data not shown). Therefore, all subsequent assays were performed at 15°C. At this lower temperature, the rate of uptake of [ 14 C]pyruvate by E. coli MG1655 Δ btsT containing the control plasmid pBAD24 was approximately linear for 60 s ( Fig. 3 ). Cells transformed with pBAD24- btsT displayed a 6-fold higher linear uptake rate for 30 s and reached 4-fold higher steady state (0.5 nmol/mg of protein) at about 40 s. Transport rates in intact cells of E. coli MG1655 Δ btsT transformed with pBAD24- btsT were also measured at various external pH values (Fig. S6). The highest pyruvate uptake rate was found at pH 7.5, the pH value of the buffer used in all of the subsequent experiments. The pyruvate uptake rate decreased at pH 6, and no transport was detected at pH 4.5 or pH 3.0.

In addition, we tested pyruvate uptake by BtsT in intact cells, which have a decreased pyruvate metabolism. For this purpose, we used E. coli strain YYC202, which harbors mutations in the pyruvate metabolizing pathways aceEF , pflB , poxB , and pps , and therefore cannot convert pyruvate into either acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA), phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), or acetate ( 33 ). Uptake of [ 14 C]pyruvate into intact cells of E. coli YYC202 transformed with either pBAD24- btsT or pBAD24 was measured, and we observed a pattern of pyruvate uptake very similar to that in E. coli MG1655 Δ btsT transformed with these plasmids (Fig. S7). Therefore, accumulation of pyruvate could not be increased in this mutant, since it is impossible to completely block its metabolization. It is important to state here that expression of chromosomally encoded btsT is tightly controlled by the BtsS/BtsR system and therefore requires specific environmental conditions ( 9 ).
Characterization of pyruvate transport by BtsT in intact cells.
Previous observations based on the effects of electron transport chain inhibitors on pyruvate transport in E. coli K-12 suggested that the process is driven by the proton motive force ( 16 ). To test this hypothesis, we used the hydrophobic protonophores 2,4-dinitrophenol (DNP) and carbonyl cyanide m -chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) ( Fig. 4A ). Both reagents abolished substrate accumulation by BtsT, suggesting active transport of pyruvate by BtsT. To determine whether pyruvate transport by BtsT is accompanied by the movement of other ion species, several ionophores were tested. Valinomycin is a highly selective ionophore for K + ( 34 ), while nonactin forms complexes with K + , Na + , NH 4 + , and some other cations with lower affinity ( 35 ). Nigericin acts mainly as a potassium-proton antiporter but can also transport Pb + and H + into the cytosol ( 36 ). Pyruvate uptake by BtsT was not affected by any of these ionophores ( Fig. 4A ).

Additionally, the specificity of BtsT was tested by assaying several compounds with shared structural similarity for the ability to act as competitive inhibitors for the pyruvate binding site when provided in a 100-fold access ( Fig. 4B ). Br-pyruvate is a synthetic analogue of pyruvate, and was found to act as a competitive inhibitor of BtsT, which is in agreement with earlier studies in E. coli K-12 ( 16 ). None of the other compounds used ( l -malate, l -lactate, and d -lactate with a C-α hydroxyl group; phosphoenolpyruvate [PEP] with a C-α phosphoryl group; or l -alanine with a positively charged C-α amino group) reduced the rate of pyruvate uptake. We also tested monocarboxylates (acetate and propionate), a dicarboxylate (succinate), and amino acids ( l -serine and l -glycine), but the uptake rate was significantly reduced only in the presence of pyruvate or its synthetic analog Br-pyruvate, indicating the narrow specificity of the transporter. In order to determine the K m for pyruvate uptake, we quantified the initial rate of pyruvate uptake by BtsT at different concentrations of pyruvate ( Fig. 4C ). Pyruvate can enter the cells by diffusion (Fig. S8). Therefore, the uptake rates were corrected by the values determined for pure diffusion. The K m of the transporter BtsT for pyruvate measured in intact cells was 16.5 ± 6.4 μM.
Effects of Δμ̃ H+ , ΔΨ, and ΔpH on BtsT-mediated pyruvate transport.
Energization of pyruvate transport by BtsT was studied in detail in E. coli proteoliposomes by determining the consequences for pyruvate transport of an imposed electrical potential (ΔΨ) across the liposome membrane or a difference in proton concentration (ΔpH) between the interior of the liposomes and the external medium, or a combination of both, thus generating a transmembrane electrochemical proton gradient (Δμ̃ H+ ). To this end, BtsT was first expressed at high levels under the control of the pBAD promoter, affinity purified, and integrated into proteoliposomes preloaded with potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.6 ( 37 ) (Fig. S9). The proteoliposomes were then diluted 200-fold, in the presence of valinomycin, with either potassium phosphate buffer, pH 5.8 (ΔpH), potassium-free buffer, pH 7.6 (ΔΨ), potassium-free buffer, pH 5.8 (Δμ̃ H+ ), or sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.6 (Δμ̃ Na+ ), and the rates of [ 14 C]pyruvate transport were measured.
From the results, it is clear that the presence of an electrochemical H + gradient (Δμ̃ H+ ) is associated with the accumulation of [ 14 C]pyruvate in proteoliposomes and that uptake is markedly reduced in the absence of such a gradient (dilution in potassium phosphate buffer pH 7.6) ( Fig. 5A ). Thus, the initial rate of uptake of pyruvate by BtsT in the presence of Δμ̃ H+ is 27.7 nmol per mg of protein, and the steady state is achieved after about 1 min ( Fig. 5B ). Imposition of ΔpH decreased the initial rate by 2-fold, and a lower steady state (relative to Δμ̃ H+ ) was reached after 1 min. In the presence of ΔΨ, we observed an initial rate of pyruvate accumulation similar to that seen in the presence of ΔpH, but it continued to rise for the (10-min) duration of the experiment. [ 14 C]pyruvate also accumulated upon the application of Δμ̃ Na+, although the Na + ions did not stimulate initial uptake beyond the level seen with ΔpH alone ( Fig. 5A ).

Pyruvate could potentially cross the E. coli cytoplasmic membrane in its protonated form by simple diffusion ( 38 ). In the experiments where a pH gradient is generated (ΔpH, interior alkaline, exterior acidic), the [ 14 C]pyruvate is added to an extracellular environment with a low pH. Therefore, [ 14 C]pyruvate might be protonated and transported by diffusion. To rule out this possibility, liposomes without BtsT were prepared as a control (Fig. S10). Using these liposomes, we observed only a slight pyruvate accumulation. Therefore, the protonation of pyruvate at low pH does not significantly affect the outcome of these experiments.
