L11@P0: Several hyperthermophilic organisms contain an unusual phosphatase that has dual activity toward inositol
L12@P0: monophosphates and fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. The
L13@P0: structure of the second member of this family, an
L14@P0: FBPase/IMPase from Archaeoglobus fulgidus (AF2372),
L15@P0: has been solved. This enzyme shares many kinetic and
L16@P0: structural similarities with that of a previously solved
L17@P0: enzyme from Methanococcus jannaschii (MJ0109). It
L18@P0: also shows some kinetic differences in divalent metal
L19@P0: ion binding as well as structural variations at the dimer
L20@P0: interface that correlate with decreased thermal stability. The availability of different crystal forms allowed us
L21@P0: to investigate the effect of the presence of ligands on the
L22@P0: conformation of a mobile catalytic loop independently
L23@P0: of the crystal packing. This conformational variability
L24@P0: in AF2372 is compared with that observed in other members of this structural family that are sensitive or insensitive to submillimolar concentrations of Li. This analysis provides support for the previously proposed
L25@P0: mechanism of catalysis involving three metal ions. A
L26@P0: direct correlation of the loop conformation with
L27@P0: strength of Li inhibition provides a useful system of
L28@P0: classification for this extended family of enzymes.
L29@P0: In mammalian cells, inositol monophosphatases are abundant cytosolic enzymes necessary to regenerate the supply of
L30@P0: myo-inositol for the synthesis of phosphatidylinositols. However, in archaeal hyperthermophilic organisms, e.g. Archaeoglobus fulgidus, an IMPase1 ortholog is thought to be used in
L31@P0: the biosynthesis of a unique osmolyte, di-myo-inositol 1,1-phosphate (1). The archaeal enzymes have another twist as well.
L32@P0: Besides catalyzing the hydrolysis of Ins-1-P (needed in response to thermal and salt stress) they also very specifically
L33@P0: dephosphorylate FBP at C-1 (2). Enzymes with these characteristics exist as two distinct gene products in mammalian
L34@P0: systems and several bacteria (3). The dual specificity of the
L35@P0: enzyme from primitive hyperthermophiles strongly suggest
L36@P0: that IMPase and FBPase enzymes may have evolved from the
L37@P0: same gene product (2).
L38@P0: Structural features of the IMPase/FBPase (MJ0109) from
L39@P0: Methanococcus jannaschii (2) are similar to those of mammalian IMPase, inositol polyphosphase phosphatase (IPPase),
L40@P0: FBPase, and the yeast enzyme Hal2. These enzymes form an
L41@P0: extended family for which the general principles of chemical
L42@P0: reactivity are the same (3). Two or three metal ions are necessary for catalysis to occur. From the structure of human
L43@P0: IMPase with bound Ins-1-P and Gd3, it was inferred that two
L44@P0: metal ions were needed for catalysis (4). However, crystallographic data for pig kidney FBPase (5) and for MJ0109 (6) are
L45@P0: consistent with a three-metal ion-assisted catalytic mechanism.
L46@P0: There is strong biochemical evidence that Li inhibits
L47@P0: IMPase in mammalian cells where a dose response to this ion
L48@P0: correlates with a decrease in the intracellular pool of myoinositol (7). Li, by inhibiting mammalian IMPases, is thought
L49@P0: to cause a depletion of inositol that attenuates the synthesis of
L50@P0: phosphatidylinositol necessary to regenerate the phospholipase C dependent phosphatidylinositol/IP3 signaling pathway
L51@P0: (8, 9). There are two other enzymes that are very Li-sensitive
L52@P0: with known three-dimensional structures: human IPPase (10)
L53@P0: and yeast 3-phosphoadenosine-5-phosphate phosphatase,
L54@P0: Hal2 (11). In contrast, the dual specificity thermophilic
L55@P0: IMPase/FBPase enzymes from M. jannaschii and A. fulgidus
L56@P0: and mammalian FBPase are not inhibited by Li in the submillimolar range (Table I). Thus, the IMPase superfamily can
L57@P0: be functionally subdivided into those that are strongly inhibited by Li and those that are less sensitive to Li. Structural
L58@P0: hints on the mechanism of Li inhibition of human IMPase are
L59@P0: indirect. In pig kidney FBPase (13), which is inhibited by Li
L60@P0: but activated by low concentrations of K, monovalent cations
L61@P0: are thought to inhibit the enzyme by distorting the active site
L62@P0: geometry or preventing turnover or product release. Despite

L88@P1: This work presents the results of structural studies for another dual specificity IMPase/FBPase from the hyperthermophilic sulfate reducer A. fulgidus. Like MJ0109, this archaeal
L89@P1: enzyme (AF2372) has sequence features close to human
L90@P1: IMPase but key structural similarities more akin to pig kidney
L91@P1: FBPase. A comparison of the structures of the two archaeal
L92@P1: Li-insensitive enzymes (AF2372 and MJ0109), a phosphatase
L93@P1: of intermediate sensitivity (pig kidney FBPase), and three very
L94@P1: Li-sensitive members of this superfamily (human IMPase,
L95@P1: IPPase and yeast Hal2) provides a strong correlation between
L96@P1: the level of Li sensitivity and the conformation of a critical
L97@P1: catalytic loop. The disposition of this catalytic loop may serve
L98@P1: as a predictor of Li sensitivity for other IMPase family members. Furthermore, changes in the loop conformation of AF2372
L99@P1: induced by ligand binding shed some light into the possible
L100@P1: mechanism of Li inhibition in the human IMPase and into
L101@P1: how this phenomenon might have developed from the more
L102@P1: primitive archaeal enzymes.

L109@P1: L-Ins-1-P was synthesized enzymatically using an Ins-1-P synthase
L110@P1: cloned from A. fulgidus as described previously (14). The Ins-1-P and
L111@P1: the other small molecule fractions were separated from the synthase
L112@P1: with Centricon concentrators. The filtrate was evaporated under high
L113@P1: vacuum; a known amount of glucose 6-phosphate was used to quantify
L114@P1: the concentration of purified L-Ins-1-P by 31P NMR spectroscopy. Further purification of the sugar required chromatography on an AG1-X8
L115@P1: anion exchange column (elution with a gradient of 0.2–1.0 M formic
L116@P1: acid); fractions containing Ins-1-P were collected and the pH value was
L117@P1: adjusted to 7.5 with NH4OH. The purified L-Ins-1-P was stored at 4 °C
L118@P1: and used for enzymatic assays and crystal soaks. All other chemicals
L119@P1: were reagent grade.
L120@P1: Overexpression and Purification of AF2372 from A. fulgidus—Cloning the AF2372 gene from A. fulgidus and overexpression of the gene
L121@P1: product in Escherichia coli were carried out as described elsewhere (15).
L122@P1: Key steps of the purification procedure included heating of crude protein at 85 °C for 30 min, followed by chromatography on Q-Sepharose
L123@P1: fast flow (elution with a linear gradient of 0 to 0.5 M KCl). Column
L124@P1: fractions were monitored for IMPase activity using 15 mM pNPP and 30
L125@P1: mM MgCl2 in 50 mM Tris and 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5. Purity of the fractions
L126@P1: was assessed with SDS-PAGE; fractions that were greater than 90%
L127@P1: pure were collected, pooled, concentrated 10-fold, and dialyzed against
L128@P1: two changes of 50 mM Tris, pH 7.5. The concentrated solution was then
L129@P1: loaded onto a 1.6  72-cm Sephacryl S-200HR gel filtration column
L130@P1: pre-equilibrated with 50 mM Tris and 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.5. Fractions
L131@P1: containing pure Ins-1-Pase activity (as measured by SDS-PAGE and
L132@P1: silver staining of the gels) were concentrated to 12 mg/ml and stored in
L133@P1: 20 mM Tris HCl and 1.0 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, until used for
L134@P1: crystallization.
L135@P1: Activity Assays—Production of inorganic phosphate from FBP and
L136@P1: Ins-1-P was monitored using a colorimetric assay (16). Substrate concentration was typically 2 mM (except for pNPP, which was 0.5 mM) in
L137@P1: 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, with varying amounts of divalent cations (Mg2 or
L138@P1: Mn2) added. The solution with substrate and enzyme was heated to 85
L139@P1: or 80 °C (the temperature is indicated with each assay set) for 1 min,
L140@P1: then 900 l of dye was added, and the OD660 was measured. A standard
L141@P1: Pi curve was used to convert OD660 to mol of Pi produced. Assays
L142@P1: typically used 1.0–2.0 g of enzyme.
L143@P1: Crystallization—AF2372 was crystallized by vapor diffusion using
L144@P1: hanging drops of 5 l. The apoenzyme was crystallized in either 0.2 M
L145@P1: ammonium nitrate and 30% PEG 3350 (P32 form) or 0.2 M dihydrogen
L146@P1: ammonium phosphate and 30% PEG 3350 (P21 form). Enzyme solution
L147@P1: (2.5 l of 10 mg/ml) was mixed with an equal volume of mother liquor
L148@P1: and equilibrated using the vapor diffusion hanging drop method. To
L149@P1: monitor the catalytic loop conformation under various conditions, the
L150@P1: apoenzyme crystals were soaked in solutions containing ligands and
L151@P1: different divalent cations. In addition, the enzyme was co-crystallized
L152@P1: with Ca2 (50 mM) using 5–7% v/v solution of PEG 8000 in 50 mM Tris,
L153@P1: pH 8.0, with 100 mM NaCl. These crystals were then soaked with either
L154@P1: D-Ins-1-P or FBP. The drops were equilibrated against the reservoir
L155@P1: with the concentration of PEG 8000 between 5 and 7%. Crystals appeared in few days and grew to full size within a week.
L156@P1: X-ray Data Collection—Crystals having approximate dimensions of
L157@P1: 0.5  0.4  0.4 mm were mounted in glass capillaries directly from the
L158@P1: crystallization wells, or flash frozen in a stream of nitrogen gas. Diffraction data were collected both at cryo conditions and at room temperature. Data were collected at Boston University School of Medicine
L159@P1: at room temperature on a Rigaku RU-300 rotating anode generator
L160@P1: with an R-AXIS IIC imaging plate detector. Data were also collected at
L161@P1: Rice University using a Rigaku H3R rotating anode generator with an

L165@P1: was indexed and reduced using DENZO and Scalepack (see Ref. 17),
L166@P1: while the data collected at Rice University was indexed and reduced
L167@P1: using Crystal Clear software. In addition to the apoenzyme (P32 or P21),
L168@P1: whose crystals diffracted to 2 Å, several data sets were collected on
L169@P1: crystals soaked with ligands or co-crystallized complexes. The data
L170@P1: were collected to 2.2 Å resolution for the ligand soaks.
L171@P1: Structural Solution and Refinement—The phase problem was solved
L172@P1: by using the molecular replacement method with the MJ0109 model
L173@P1: (PDB code 1DK4) as a probe structure. The rotational as well translational searches were performed using the program Amore (18). The

L175@P1: was refined by rigid body refinement and simulated annealing (crystallography NMR software) with manual rebuilding using O (19). The

L178@P1: and found to be nearly perfectly twinned with a twinning ratio of 0.499.

L181@P1: the program Xplor (21) lowered the R-factor significantly. Several macrocycles including manual rebuilding sessions done on the SGI work
L182@P1: station with the program Xtalview (22) led to convergence. The final
L183@P1: models were refined by the program Shelxl-97 (23). The models were
L184@P1: evaluated using Procheck (24). Table II provides a summary of data

L599@P9: DISCUSSION
L600@P9: There have been a number of studies directed at elucidating
L601@P9: the details of Li effects on the IMPase superfamily. A series of
L602@P9: combined mutational, spectroscopic, and kinetic experiments
L603@P9: carried out on the human IMPase implicated two residues,
L604@P9: Cys-218 and His-217, in Li inhibition (8). Both residues are
L605@P9: remote from the metal ion binding site and, if confirmed as part
L606@P9: of the Li binding site, they cannot be involved in catalysis.
L607@P9: There was speculation that these residues could influence the
L608@P9: binding of the metal ion at site 2, which would hinder the
L609@P9: release of phosphate and result in inhibition of IMPase activity
L610@P9: (8). This proposed mechanism was termed the “bumping hypothesis.” Two similar mutations were made in one of the
L611@P9: Li-insensitive archaeal IMPase/FBPase enzymes (MJ0109).
L612@P9: Mutation of Arg-198 in the M. jannaschii enzyme (aligned with
L613@P9: His-217 in the mammalian enzyme) to His led to complete loss
L614@P9: of IMPase activity (25). However, introduction of cysteine in
L615@P9: place of Ala-199 (which aligned with Cys-218 of the human
L616@P9: IMPase) increased the sensitivity of MJ0109 to Li (IC50 decreased from 150 to  80 mM (25)). Such a relatively small
L617@P9: change in Li sensitivity might be interpreted as stemming
L618@P9: from other effects. It is more likely that elements conferring
L619@P9: Li sensitivity involve secondary structural elements that require more than a point mutation.
L620@P9: Random mutagenesis studies carried out on the product of
L621@P9: the yeast Hal2 gene provided other hypotheses. The Hal2 gene
L622@P9: is an in vivo target of Na and Li toxicity, and its overexpression improves the tolerance of higher plants to salt. This enzyme is differentially sensitive to monovalent cations: tolerant
L623@P9: of high sodium concentrations but inhibited by lithium. Mutating residues Val-170 and Trp-293 or one of the residues forming
L624@P9: a salt bridge between Glu-238 and Arg-152 caused a marked
L625@P9: decrease in Li sensitivity (11). It was speculated that the salt
L626@P9: bridge between Arg-152 to Asp-263 might be stabilized by an
L627@P9: ionic network involving monovalent ion binding. The hydrophobic interaction between Val-170 and Trp-292 was required for
L628@P9: an effective formation of the catalytic core that, when disrupted, changed sensitivity to Na and Li more than catalytic
L629@P9: activity. Mutations at these positions shifted the IC50 for wild
L630@P9: type Hal2 from 0.07 to 4 mM in E238K mutants and to 0.5 mM
L631@P9: in the V70A mutant.
L632@P9: In the case of human IMPase, a two-metal ion-assisted catalytic mechanism was proposed. Li binding was proposed to
L633@P9: interfere with the second metal ion binding site (4), in effect,
L634@P9: preventing product release. In the case of archaeal IMPases, we

L645@P9: The direct consequence of this mechanism is the following
L646@P9: hypothesis for Li inhibition. The mobile loop is important for
L647@P9: localization and binding of the third metal ion, and this metal

L653@P9: into the active site. The Mg2 ionic radius is 0.66 Å. Therefore,
L654@P9: only Li is small enough (0.68 Å) to substitute structurally for
L655@P9: the Mg2 ion. However, because of its charge it would be unable
L656@P9: to create a nucleophile, thus inhibiting the enzyme. The much
L657@P9: larger Na or K ions (0.97 Å and 1.33 Å, respectively) would

L659@P9: Mg2. As is suggested by the difference in ionic radii they must
L660@P9: have much lower affinity for the enzyme site. Only at exceedingly high concentrations would they cause significant inhibition. The existence of conformational classes for the mobile loop

L662@P9: inhibition. The representative enzymes with a smaller and
L663@P9: more compact mobile loop, like AF2372, respond to Li in a
L664@P9: much more subdued manner than enzymes with longer and
L665@P9: more flexible catalytic loop. Therefore, the IMPase superfamily
L666@P9: can be subdivided into two distinct classes as defined by a key
L667@P9: structural feature (mobile loop conformation) and its functional
L668@P9: importance (Li inhibitory properties).
