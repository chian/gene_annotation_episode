Viable but nonculturable (VBNC) bacteria do not form colonies in standard medium but otherwise retain their metabolic activity and can express toxic proteins. Many bacterial genera, including Escherichia , Vibrio , and Listeria , have been shown to enter the VBNC state upon exposure to adverse conditions, such as low temperature, radiation, and starvation. Ultimately, these organisms pose a public health risk with potential implications for the pharmaceutical and food industries, as dormant organisms are especially difficult to selectively eliminate and VBNC bacteria can be resuscitated if placed in an environment with appropriate nutrition and temperature. Here we used a microfluidic system to monitor the resuscitation of single VBNC cells over time. We provide new molecular insights into the initiation of resuscitation by demonstrating that VBNC E. coli cells rapidly take up pyruvate with an inducible high-affinity transporter, whose expression is triggered by the BtsSR-YpdAB sensing network.
KEYWORDS: BtsS, histidine kinase, microfluidics, proteomics, pyruvate transporter
ABSTRACT
Escherichia coli and many other bacterial species can enter into a viable but nonculturable (VBNC) state, which is a survival strategy adopted by cells exposed to adverse environmental conditions. Pyruvate is known to be one factor that promotes resuscitation of VBNC cells. Here we studied the role of a pyruvate-sensing network, composed of the histidine kinase-response regulator systems BtsS/BtsR and YpdA/YpdB and the target gene btsT , encoding the high-affinity pyruvate/H + symporter BtsT, in the resuscitation of VBNC E. coli K-12 cells after exposure to cold for 120 days. Analysis of the proteome of VBNC cells revealed upregulation, relative to exponentially growing cells, of BtsT and other proteins involved in pyruvate metabolism. Provision of pyruvate stimulated protein and DNA biosynthesis, and thus resuscitation, in wild-type but not btsSR ypdAB mutant VBNC cells. This result was corroborated by time-dependent tracking of the resuscitation of individual VBNC E. coli cells observed in a microfluidic system. Finally, transport assays revealed that 14 C-labeled pyruvate was rapidly taken up into VBNC cells by BtsT. These results provide the first evidence that pyruvate is taken up as a carbon source for the resuscitation of VBNC E. coli cells.

INTRODUCTION
Viable but nonculturable (VBNC) bacteria are deep dormant phenotypic variants that are characterized by a loss of culturability in conventional culture media but maintain viability markers ( 1 ). Thus, production of biomass, transport of nutrients, active metabolism, membrane integrity, and respiration are all detectable in these dormant populations. Cells enter the VBNC state in response to natural stresses, such as starvation, low temperature, high osmolarity, or radiation ( 2 ). These environmental stresses could potentially kill whole populations unless at least some cells enter this dormant state. Since VBNC cells represent a strategy for survival under adverse environmental conditions ( 3 , 4 ), they are difficult to eliminate with antibiotics, due to their low levels of metabolism. VBNC cells have been reported for many bacterial species ( 5 , 6 ), and the mechanisms underlying the induction of this state have been studied extensively over the past two decades in, for example, Vibrio species ( 7 – 9 ), Salmonella typhi ( 10 ), Campylobacter jejuni ( 11 ), Lactobacillus acetotolerans ( 12 ), Yersinia pestis ( 13 ), and pathogenic Escherichia coli strains ( 14 ).
VBNC cells can, however, resume cell division. The process of reestablishing culturability is termed resuscitation, and its underlying mechanisms are still unclear. However, some factors that promote the restoration of culturability have been identified, e.g., YeaZ promoting factor, catalase, α-ketoglutarate, and pyruvate ( 9 , 15 – 17 ). Several studies have described pyruvate as the principal promoter of resuscitation, albeit in its function as an antioxidant ( 2 , 17 , 18 ). Pyruvate is known to scavenge hydrogen peroxide ( 19 , 20 ) and the hydroxyl radical ( 21 ), and it prevents lipid peroxidation ( 22 ). Pyruvate and other α-ketoacids scavenge oxygen radicals through a nonenzymatic oxidative decarboxylation mechanism, which produces water and the corresponding acid (acetic acid in the case of pyruvate) ( 15 , 23 ).
During both the induction and resuscitation processes, bacteria must monitor the extracellular environment. E. coli and many other organisms use two-component systems (TCSs) for environmental sensing and cellular responses ( 24 ). A membrane-integrated histidine kinase perceives the stimulus, and a cytosolic response regulator mediates an appropriate output. We previously carried out several studies of two of these TCSs in E. coli , i.e., the BtsS/BtsR and YpdA/YpdB systems ( 25 – 30 ) ( Fig. 1 ). Both TCSs respond to extracellular pyruvate, although with different affinities, and thus constitute a pyruvate-sensing network, BtsSR/YpdAB. YpdA/YpdB activation results in yhjX expression, whereas BtsS/BtsR activation promotes btsT expression. Recently, the corresponding protein BtsT was identified as a high-affinity pyruvate/H + symporter ( 29 ); yhjX codes for a transporter of currently unknown function ( 25 ).

Here we present a comprehensive study of the role of pyruvate sensing and transport during resuscitation of one of the most commonly used E. coli laboratory strains, MG1655, following extended cold stress. We demonstrate that pyruvate uptake by BtsT is crucial for the resuscitation of VBNC E. coli cells. In addition, we use microfluidics as a valuable method for tracking individual VBNC E. coli cells during the resuscitation process. Throughout this study, we basically compare three strains, i.e., the E. coli MG1655 wild-type (WT) strain, the E. coli MG1655 Δ btsSR Δ ypdAB ( btsSR ypdAB ) mutant, and the trans -complemented mutant. The mutant lacks the pyruvate signaling network, which prevents expression of the transporters BtsT and YhjX.
RESULTS
Characterization of the VBNC state in cold-stressed E. coli cells.
The E. coli MG1655 WT strain and the isogenic btsSR ypdAB mutant were grown in lysogeny broth (LB) until the exponential growth phase, and cells were then transferred into fresh LB and stored at 4°C. Culturability decreased rapidly over the first 10 days and subsequently declined at a much lower rate ( Fig. 2 ). After 120 days, culturability had been completely lost in both strains ( Fig. 2 ). No significant difference between the two strains was observed in terms of the decreases in the numbers of CFU. To assess viability, the membrane potential and respiratory activity of these strains were assayed ( Table 1 ). After 120 days of incubation at 4°C, only around 8% of WT cells and 9% of btsSR ypdAB cells exhibited a detectable membrane potential, as ascertained by flow cytometry. The remaining cells had no measurable membrane potential and therefore were dead. Respiratory activity, assayed as the capacity of cells to reduce 5-cyano-2,3-ditolyl tetrazolium chloride (CTC), decreased to levels in the same range ( Table 1 ). These results reveal that comparable fractions of the WT and btsSR ypdAB mutant populations remained viable under these conditions but were not culturable. There was no difference in total cell numbers between the two strains, as judged by the total protein content ( Table 1 ).

To further characterize the VBNC E. coli cells, we isolated the viable cell fraction by density gradient centrifugation with Percoll ( 31 , 32 ), performed a proteomic analysis using mass spectrometry (MS), and compared the results with those for freshly grown mid-log-phase E. coli cells ( Fig. 3 ). Among the most pronounced differences were significantly increased levels in VBNC cells of proteins associated with the cold shock response (e.g., 250-, 132-, 125-, and 80-fold increases for YdfK, CspB, CspI, and CspG, respectively), glycerol-3-phosphate, disaccharide and pyrimidine catabolism (e.g., 37- to 11-fold increases for GlpABC, GlpFK, LacZ, and PreT), and pyruvate metabolism (e.g., 11-, 5.6-, and 4.6-fold increases for LldD, PflA, and PckA, respectively). The significance of pyruvate was corroborated by the significant upregulation of the high-affinity pyruvate transporter BtsT, relative to its level in log-phase E. coli cultures. Conversely, many proteins associated with metabolism (e.g., AldB, AdhP, SdaB, PrpC, and LdhA), cell division (e.g., CedA), and tRNA modification (TtcA), as well as stress responses (UspE, UspF, PsiF, Fnr, and Crl), were found to be downregulated ( Fig. 3 ). These alterations of the proteome are in accord with the changes expected upon long-term storage of E. coli in the cold with nutrient limitation. The results are in line with previous proteomic analyses of VBNC E. coli and other species ( 14 , 33 , 34 ).

Role of the BtsSR and YpdAB systems in the resuscitation of VBNC E. coli cells.
After successfully inducing the VBNC state by long-term storage in the cold, we investigated the resuscitation of these cells in different media ( Table 2 ). We found that VBNC cells required an amino-acid-rich complex medium, and supplementation with 2 mM pyruvate plus its isotonic dilution (10-fold diluted LB [dLB] with unchanged NaCl concentration) significantly increased the number of resuscitating cells. These results suggest a role of the BtsR/BtsR- and YpdA/YpdB-mediated activation of btsT and yhjX , respectively, which occurs when nutrients are depleted and extracellular pyruvate is available ( 27 ). Therefore, 10-fold dLB was used throughout these studies. VBNC E. coli cells were resuspended in this medium and incubated at 25°C, with agitation. In the absence of any additive (control), small numbers of culturable cells of the WT strain but essentially none of the btsSR ypdAB mutants grew out as colonies ( Fig. 4 , Control). In addition to the btsSR ypdAB mutant, a third strain was used, in which the two disabled operons were complemented by the pCOLA- btsSR-ypdAB plasmid. This last strain behaved like the WT strain under these conditions.

When external pyruvate was present to promote resuscitation, the number of culturable WT cells increased significantly, whereas the btsSR ypdAB mutant showed a basal level (less than 5%) of resuscitation ( Fig. 4 , Pyruvate). The trans -complemented mutant exhibited a resuscitation profile resembling that of the WT strain ( Fig. 4 , green and blue bars). In parallel, the efficacy of α-ketoglutarate as a resuscitation-promoting compound was tested; this compound was found to promote resuscitation of all three strains ( Fig. 4 , α-ketoglutarate). As expected from previous studies, the addition of acetate did not promote resuscitation ( Fig. 4 , Acetate). These results demonstrate that resuscitation of cold-stressed E. coli VBNC cells in the presence of pyruvate requires the BtsSR-YpdAB signaling network.
To corroborate these findings, the biosynthetic activity of the three strains was analyzed by monitoring the incorporation of [ methyl - 3 H]thymidine, as a marker for DNA synthesis ( Fig. 5A ), and 35 S-labeled methionine-cysteine, as a marker for protein synthesis ( Fig. 5B ). As described above, VBNC cells were incubated at 37°C in dLB supplemented with 2 mM pyruvate, and biosynthesis rates were measured in the early stage of resuscitation. Within the first 40 min, increases in both DNA and protein biosynthesis were observed for the WT strain and the complemented strain. Similarly, the presence of α-ketoglutarate stimulated the incorporation of radiolabeled methionine-cysteine and thymidine in the WT strain (see Fig. S1 in the supplemental material). In stark contrast, the btsSR ypdAB mutant incorporated only very low levels of the radiolabeled compounds, indicating an extremely low rate of metabolic activity in these cells under the tested resuscitation conditions.

Time-resolved resuscitation of single VBNC E. coli cells in microfluidics.
To explore the behavior of individual cells in the population during resuscitation, we performed time-lapse imaging of VBNC cells undergoing resuscitation. We used a microfluidic system ( 35 ) that maintained constant environmental conditions (e.g., temperature and oxygen levels) and simultaneously allowed us to observe the resuscitation of single cells from the VBNC state with high temporal resolution ( Fig. 6A to D ).

When cells were loaded into the chambers (time zero), basically two types of morphologically distinct cells were observed. One type was small and clear, which are features typical of dead cells. The second cell type was darker, longer, and thicker, thus resembling intact E. coli cells. The latter cells are referred to as “potentially” dividing cells. Chambers filled with WT and btsSR ypdAB mutant strains were then flushed with dLB without any additives. Cell lysis was observed for both tested strains, and no cells succeeded in undergoing cell division (see Movies S1 and S2 in the supplemental material).
Next, we tested the ability of both the WT strain and the btsSR ypdAB mutant strain to resuscitate in the chambers when dLB was supplemented with pyruvate (Movies S3 and S4). As expected, the WT strain resumed cell division. On average, the first division occurred after about 2 h of incubation ( Fig. 6E ). In the case of the btsSR ypdAB mutant, no cell division was observed ( Fig. 6E ); when the trans -complemented mutant was tested, however, resuscitation like that for the WT strain was observed ( Fig. 6E ; also see Movies S5 and S6). We observed that the process of resuscitation was slightly faster in the complemented strain than in the WT strain, since the first cell division of the complemented mutant occurred within the first hour ( Fig. 6E ). The complemented strain harbors the genes encoding the signaling systems on a plasmid (with gene expression relying only on the leakiness of the promoter), which presumably influences the resuscitation of VBNC cells, a phenomenon that has been observed in other studies ( 36 – 38 ).
To analyze quantitatively the resuscitation in microfluidic chambers, we counted the “potentially” dividing cells at time zero (among a sample of 702 cells in a total of 150 chambers) and manually monitored these cells for division. The division of 1 VBNC cell was recorded as 1 resuscitation event, and subsequent division of a daughter cell was not counted as a resuscitation event. We found that 24% ± 3% WT cells and 35% ± 4% of cells of the complemented mutant divided and formed microcolonies ( Fig. 6F ). Essentially no resuscitation was observed in the absence of pyruvate, but about 10% of cells grew into microcolonies in the case of the complemented strain under these conditions, suggesting that the signaling network might affect some other processes that are thus far unknown. The results of these single-cell studies are in complete agreement with those of the population-based assays described above, and both reveal the importance of the BtsSR-YpdAB pyruvate-sensing network in promoting resuscitation of cold-stressed E. coli cells from the VBNC state.
Rapid uptake of pyruvate by VBNC cells.
Having demonstrated that the BtsSR and YpdAB systems are crucial for resuscitation from the VBNC state in the presence of pyruvate, we tested the capacity of VBNC cells to take up pyruvate. It is known that pyruvate plays a role in this process as a scavenger of reactive oxygen species (ROS), but little is known about its role as a metabolizable carbon source in this context. To address this question, we measured the uptake of radiolabeled [ 14 C]pyruvate into WT and btsSR ypdAB VBNC cells. Uptake rates were determined by using the rapid filtration method ( 29 ). Cells were taken from the cold (4°C), transferred into fresh LB at 15°C, and immediately mixed with [ 14 C]pyruvate (80 µM). Within 20 s, the WT strain was able to take up pyruvate at a rate of 0.33 nmol of pyruvate per mg of protein ( Fig. 7 ). After this initial rapid increase of radiolabeled [ 14 C]pyruvate in WT cells, a plateau was reached at about 30 s, which presumably represents an equilibrium between uptake and concurrent metabolism of pyruvate. In contrast, no pyruvate uptake was observed in the btsSR ypdAB mutant ( Fig. 7 ). These results demonstrate rapid uptake of pyruvate by E. coli VBNC cells, suggesting that pyruvate functions as a carbon source during early resuscitation. Furthermore, our result suggests that pyruvate uptake is mediated by a high-affinity pyruvate transporter, such as BtsT (Fig. S2).

DISCUSSION
Entry into the VBNC state enables pathogens and nonpathogens to survive in adverse environments but poses health risks in clinical settings and for the food industry and water supplies, once such cells become culturable again ( 2 ). More than 100 species of bacteria have been shown to enter the nonculturable state, but only a subset of those have been shown to resuscitate, because the molecular mechanisms that initiate the process are not yet well understood ( 6 ). Among other factors, pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate are important for resuscitation, because they are effective ROS scavengers. This study demonstrates for the first time that pyruvate is avidly taken up by starved and cold-stressed E. coli VBNC cells. Uptake of pyruvate under these conditions is mediated by the high-affinity transporter BtsT, whose expression is under the control of the pyruvate-sensing BtsSR-YpdAB network ( Fig. 1 ). Thus, far, this pyruvate-sensing network has been found to be important for the equalization, in the late exponential stage of growth, of the physiological state of all cells within an E. coli population. During the transition to the stationary phase, this network enables individual cells to take up pyruvate according to their needs ( 30 ). The studies presented here indicate that VBNC cells that lack this network are essentially unable to resuscitate in the presence of pyruvate, as confirmed by their inability to resume DNA and protein biosynthesis. However, resuscitation can be rescued by providing the genes for the pyruvate-sensing network in trans . In addition, the mutant and the complemented strain are resuscitated in the presence of α-ketoglutarate, just like the parental strain. α-Ketoglutarate is an efficient ROS scavenger, and E. coli has the constitutively produced α-ketoglutarate transporter KgtP ( 39 ), so that VBNC cells independent of a mutation in btsSR ypdAB might profit from this compound.
Our proteomic study revealed that VBNC E. coli cells are characterized by significantly increased copy numbers for the high-affinity pyruvate/H + symporter BtsT ( Fig. 3 ). Consequently, WT VBNC cells were able to take up pyruvate within seconds of its provision ( Fig. 7 ).
Pyruvate forms a node between the tricarboxylic acid cycle, fatty acid synthesis (both via acetyl coenzyme A [acetyl-CoA]), and biosynthesis of amino acids, such as alanine, and oxaloacetate, which feeds into gluconeogenesis. At low oxygen concentrations, pyruvate dehydrogenase is replaced by either pyruvate formate lyase or pyruvate ferredoxin oxidoreductase, which results in acetyl-CoA production without formation of NADH and permits pyruvate to be reduced to lactate. In fact, several enzymes involved in pyruvate metabolism are strongly upregulated in the proteome of VBNC E. coli cells, relative to mid-log-phase cells ( Fig. 3 ), including l -lactate dehydrogenase (LldD), pyruvate formate lyase (PflA), and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PckA). Some of the imported pyruvate might be immediately cleaved to acetyl-CoA and formate by PflA, and another fraction might be utilized by PckA for gluconeogenesis. Moreover, pyruvate might be reduced to lactate, which is then oxidized by LldD, with electrons being fed into the electron transport chain. The proteomic study also revealed significant but less dramatic changes (3-fold decreases) in the levels of PtsG and PtsH, the major components of the phosphotransferase system (PTS)-specific glucose uptake system in VBNC E. coli . It should be noted that the copy numbers of these two proteins per cell are rather high (2,775 and 24,850 copies/cell for PtsG and PtsH, respectively) in exponentially growing cells and that a decrease of about 50% is already observed in 3-day-old stationary-phase cultures ( 40 ). These findings suggest that the uptake of glucose, which requires phosphorylation in the first step, is downregulated in starving cells and pyruvate becomes the preferred carbon source.
The results described here correlate well with those of an earlier study, in which Salmonella was shown to resuscitate in the presence of pyruvate but not when supplied with the pyruvate analogue bromopyruvate or phenylpyruvate, both of which possess the α-keto carboxy residue ( 17 ). It is still unclear whether these analogues can be taken up, but they are certainly not metabolized. Salmonella also contains the pyruvate-responsive system BtsSR and the transporter BtsT but lacks the YpdAB system ( 45 ). Pyruvate fermentation has also been shown to be crucial for the long-term survival of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in the absence of oxygen ( 42 ). In Rhodopseudomonas palustris , longevity under nutrient-depleted conditions is ensured not only by rearrangement of the metabolic pathways but also by upregulation of various high-affinity transporters ( 43 ). These examples indicate that bacteria adapt their transport capacities and metabolism for utilization of alternative carbon sources to ensure long-term survival.
In this study, we used microfluidic devices to directly monitor resuscitation of cold-stressed VBNC E. coli cells ( Fig. 6 ). These devices provide optimal and controlled growth conditions for each single bacterium, avoiding misinterpretation of bacterial behavior based on heterogeneous cultivation conditions ( 44 ). It was possible to follow the time-resolved resuscitation of individual cells (see Movies S1 to S6 in the supplemental material). While we focused primarily on the effect of pyruvate, we observed that resuscitation of a cell is accompanied by marked changes in volume within minutes after exposure to pyruvate, a phenomenon that needs to be explored in more detail. All resuscitating cells that resumed division first underwent an increase in volume. However, some cells that gained in volume were unable to divide and died.
In summary, several studies have highlighted pyruvate as one of the main factors that promote resuscitation ( 15 ). Although its role as a ROS scavenger is known ( 41 ), this study reveals that VBNC E. coli cells take up pyruvate via the high-affinity transporter BtsT. Cells then initiate DNA and protein biosynthesis and eventually divide and grow out as colonies. The pyruvate-sensing histidine kinase response regulator systems BtsS/BtsR and YpdA/YpdB are widely distributed among gammaproteobacteria ( 45 ). They both belong to the LytS/LytTR-type histidine kinase-response regulator systems, whose importance for pyruvate uptake has recently been demonstrated in Bacillus subtilis ( 46 ). Therefore, these systems, which fine-tune pyruvate uptake in bacteria under adverse environmental conditions, might be much more important than currently thought.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Strains, plasmids, and oligonucleotides.
In this study, we used the E. coli strains MG1655 ( 47 ) and MG1655 Δ btsSR Δ ypdAB ( 30 ). For complementation in trans of the mutant strain, the plasmid pCOLA- btsSR-ypdAB was constructed by inserting btsSR (into the BamHI and NotI sites) and ypdAB (into the NdeI and XhoI sites) in the pCOLA-Duet-1 vector (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). All oligonucleotide sequences used for strain construction are available on request.
Molecular biological techniques.
Plasmid DNA and genomic DNA were isolated using the HiYield plasmid minikit (Suedlaborbedarf) and the DNeasy blood and tissue kit (Qiagen), respectively. DNA fragments for plasmid construction were amplified from genomic DNA by PCR. DNA fragments were purified from agarose gels using a HiYield PCR cleanup and gel extraction kit (Suedlaborbedarf). Q5 DNA polymerase (New England BioLabs) was used according to the supplier’s instructions. Restriction enzymes and other DNA-modifying enzymes were also purchased from New England BioLabs and used according to the manufacturer’s directions.
Growth conditions.
E. coli MG1655 strains were grown overnight in LB (10 g/liter tryptone, 5 g/liter yeast extract, 10 g/liter NaCl). After inoculation, bacteria were routinely grown in LB, with agitation (200 rpm), at the designated temperature. For solid medium, 1.5% (wt/vol) agar was added. Where appropriate, media were supplemented with antibiotics (50 µg/ml kanamycin sulfate).
Induction of the VBNC state.
An overnight culture of the E. coli MG1655 strains was used to inoculate LB, and cells were grown to an optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) of 1.2. Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation and washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (8.1 mM Na 2 HPO 4 , 1.47 mM KH 2 PO 4 , 137 mM NaCl, 2.68 mM KCl [pH 8.25]). Cells were then resuspended in LB to a final density of 10 10 cells/ml and were stored (without shaking) at 4°C, to induce long-term cold stress.
Viability assays.
The CTC reduction assay was carried out with the reagents included in the Bacstain CTC rapid staining kit (Dojindo Kumamote, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This method assays levels of bacterial aerobic respiration ( 48 ). In the presence of an active electron transport chain, CTC is reduced to the insoluble and fluorescent (red) salt formazan, which can be detected using fluorescence microscopy. Samples of VBNC cultures were taken and diluted with PBS to a final cell density of 10 6 cells/ml. For each 1 ml of cell suspension, 20 μl of CTC solution and 1 μl of enhancing reagent A were added. Bacterial suspensions were incubated in the dark at 37°C for 30 min. The percentage of fluorescent cells was then determined by flow cytometry.
Alternatively, the viability of VBNC cells was checked by using the BacLight bacterial membrane potential kit (Invitrogen). 3,3-Diethyloxacarbocyanine [DiOC 2 (3)] exhibits green fluorescence in all bacterial cells, but the fluorescence shifts toward red emission as the dye molecules self-associate in the membranes of cells that are able to maintain a high membrane potential. Therefore, the ratio of red to green fluorescence serves as a measure of cell viability. To 1-ml aliquots containing approximately 10 6 cells, 10 μl of 3 mM DiOC 2 (3) was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for at least 20 min. Cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry.
Flow cytometry.
Aliquots of fluorescently labeled VBNC cells were taken, diluted 1:1,000 in PBS, and analyzed using a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer equipped with a solid-state laser (excitation at 488 nm; 20 mW). Forward-angle light scatter (FSC) and side-angle light scatter (SSC) were measured using the corresponding detectors.
The red fluorescence emission was quantified with the FL2 filter (BP 585/40 filter), and the green fluorescence emission was collected with the FL1 filter (BP 533/30 filter). The sheath flow rate was 14 µl/min, and no more than 100,000 events/s were acquired. For each sample run, a total of 10,000 events were collected. Analysis of data was carried out using Cytospec software ( http://www.cyto.purdue.edu/Purdue_software ).
Resuscitation from the VBNC state.
VBNC cells (100 ml) were collected by centrifugation (5,000 × g , 10 min), resuspended in prewarmed dLB (0.1× LB with 10 g/liter NaCl), dLB supplemented with 2 mM pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate, or acetate, LB, LB supplemented with 2 mM pyruvate, or M9 minimal medium supplemented with 2 mM pyruvate, and incubated at 37°C for 5 h. The cells were then plated on LB agar plates, and the CFU were counted.
Quantification of protein and DNA biosynthesis during resuscitation.
After centrifugation, E. coli VBNC cells were resuspended to 10 8 cells/ml in dLB with 2 mM pyruvate. These cell suspensions were aliquoted (1 ml each) and incubated at 37°C with 1 µl of either l -[ 35 S]methionine– l -[ 35 S]cysteine (10 mCi/ml, >1,000 Ci/mmol; Biotrend Chemikalien AG) or [ methyl - 3 H]thymidine (1 mCi/ml, 2 Ci/mol; Moravek, Inc.). At the indicated time points, aliquots were withdrawn and cells were denatured by the addition of 1 ml of ice-cold 50% (wt/vol) trichloric acid (TCA) in PBS. Samples were vortex-mixed and incubated on ice for 15 min to extract and to remove the acid-soluble materials. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged at 16,500 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Pellets were washed twice with ice-cold TCA-PBS [(10% (wt/vol)] and finally dissolved in 10 µl 0.1 M NaOH. Subsequently, the pH was neutralized with drops of HCl. All samples were transferred into scintillation vials containing scintillation fluid (MP Biomedicals), and radioactivity was measured in a liquid scintillation counter (Perkin-Elmer). The same protocol was applied to E. coli MG1655 cells in mid-log phase, which served as the positive control. Incorporation of [ 3 H]thymidine and [ 35 S]methionine-[ 35 S]cysteine during resuscitation was taken as a measure of biological activity, and values are expressed as a percentage of the positive control value.
Pyruvate transport assay.
To assay for pyruvate transport, E. coli VBNC cells were resuspended at 10 10 cells/ml in fresh LB at 15°C (transport assays for [3- 14 C]pyruvate have to be performed at 15°C to minimize its metabolization, which would result in diffusion of 14 C-labeled CO 2 out of the cells). Subsequently, 32 µl [ 14 C]pyruvate (10 Ci/mol; Biotrend) was added to 200 μl of cell suspension, resulting in a final substrate concentration of 80 μM. Cells were incubated at 15°C, and transport was terminated at the indicated times by the addition of 2.5 ml 100 mM LiCl, 100 mM KH 2 PO 4 (pH 6.0) (stop buffer), followed by rapid filtration through membrane filters (MN GF-5, 0.4 μm; Macherey-Nagel). The filters were washed three times and dissolved in 4.5 ml of scintillation fluid (MP Biomedicals), and the radioactivity (in counts per minute) was determined in a liquid scintillation counter (PerkinElmer).
Microfluidics.
The microfluidic experiments were performed in polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)-based devices mounted on the stage of a Nikon Ti-E Eclipse fluorescence microscopy system (Nikon Corp., Japan). Phase-contrast images were taken using an Andor Luca R charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Andor Technology, Northern Ireland) in combination with an 100× objective (Plan Apochromat λ Oil; numerical aperture, 1.45; working distance, 170 µm; Nikon Corp.). Images were taken every 10 min.
The microfluidic devices were fabricated using a soft lithography process, as described previously ( 49 , 50 ). The device features 50 cultivation chambers for cell culture. Each chamber is 1 µm deep and provides a cultivation area of 60 by 70 µm 2 , equivalent to a volume of 4.2 pl. The chambers are located between two 10-µm-deep channels, which serve as conduits for medium supply ( 44 ). Image processing was carried out in Fiji ( 51 ).
Sample preparation for mass spectrometry.
Aliquots of VBNC cell cultures (100 ml) were harvested and resuspended in 20 ml of PBS, and 30 ml of Percoll (GE Healthcare Sciences) was added, resulting in a final concentration of colloidal silica of 60%. Viable and dead cells were separated by several density-gradient centrifugation steps. First, the sample was centrifuged at 11,500 × g for 1 h at 4°C until two distinct cell layers were visible. The lower one was collected and resuspended in 70 ml of PBS. The cells were washed and isolated by three further centrifugation steps at 4,500 rpm for 45 min each at 4°C, and finally the pellet was resuspended in 50 µl PBS. For MS analysis, cells were disrupted by three cycles of ultrasonic treatment and subsequent quick-freezing, followed by tryptic protein digestion with the iST kit (PreOmics).
Aliquots (5 µl) of the 50-µl samples were injected into an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo) and separated in an analytical C 18 nanocolumn (25 cm by 75 µm; Aurora, Ionopticks) using a 120-min gradient from 4% to 40% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid. The effluent from the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system was directly electrosprayed into a Q Exactive HF system (Thermo). The instrument was operated in data-dependent mode, which enables automatic switching between full-scan MS and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) data acquisition. Survey full-scan MS spectra ( m/z 375 to 1,600 Th) were acquired with a resolution of 60,000 at m/z 400 Th (automatic gain control [AGC] target of 3 × 10 6 ions). The 10 most intense peptide ions with charge states between 2 and 5 were sequentially isolated to a target value of 1 × 10 5 ions and were fragmented at 27% normalized collision energy. Typical MS conditions were as follows: spray voltage, 1.5 kV; no sheath and auxiliary gas flow; heated capillary temperature, 250°C; ion selection threshold, 33,000 counts.
MaxQuant 1.5.2.8 was used to identify proteins, and these were quantified by label-free quantification (LFQ) with the following parameters: database, uniprot_Ecoli_3AUP000000625_180323; MS tolerance, 10 ppm; MS/MS tolerance, 0.5 Da; peptide false discovery rate (FDR), 0.1; protein FDR, 0.01; minimum peptide length, 5; variable modifications, oxidation (M); fixed modifications, carbamidomethyl (C); peptides for protein quantitation, razor and unique; minimum peptides, 1; minimum ratio count, 2. Identified proteins were considered upregulated or downregulated if their MaxQuant LFQ values displayed log 2 >5-fold enrichment and P values of 0.05 ( t test, adjusted for multiple comparisons), compared to the control. This analysis was performed using the Perseus platform ( 52 ).
Statistical analysis.
All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 5.03 for Windows). Data were assessed for significance between tested groups by using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison post hoc test. Error bars represent standard errors of the mean. When indicated, an unpaired t test was performed.

The MS proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE ( 53 ) partner repository ( http://www.ebi.ac.uk/pride ), with the data set identifier PXD009463.
